Setting the record straight

The thesis is broadly correct.
The scale is wildly wrong.

You've probably seen headlines claiming the internet uses 10% of global electricity. It's a satisfying narrative — tech's invisible footprint finally made visible. The problem? The number is off by a factor of six or more.

Data Centers

415 TWh used globally in 2024

That's roughly 1.5% of world electricity consumption and just 0.3% of total final energy — because electricity itself is only about 20% of all energy the world uses (the rest is heat, transport fuel, etc.). Impressive in absolute scale. Modest as a share of the whole.

Source: IEA Data Centres and Data Transmission Networks, 2024 report.

Space Cooling

~2,100 TWh consumed in 2022

Air conditioners and chillers drew five times more electricity than every data center on earth — nearly 7% of global electricity. And as climate heats, this figure is climbing fast. It is one of the fastest-growing end-uses in the entire energy system.

Source: IEA Cooling report, 2023 tracking; electricity for space cooling.

Lighting

~2,200 TWh — and shrinking

Lighting consumed approximately 8% of global electricity in 2022, but the LED revolution has bent this curve downward sharply. In 2010, lighting used over 15%. It remains, however, still five times the data center total.

Source: IEA Lighting report, Tracking Clean Energy Progress 2023.

Why it matters

Energy isn't just electricity.
And electricity isn't the story.

The confusion runs deeper than bad headlines. When people say "X% of global energy," they usually mean electricity — but electricity is only around 20% of global final energy consumption. The rest is direct combustion: gas and oil for heating buildings, diesel for trucks, coal for industrial furnaces.

This means data centers, even if they consumed 5% of electricity, would still represent just 1% of all the energy humanity burns. The distinction matters enormously for policy: decarbonizing electrons is proceeding rapidly; decarbonizing heat and transport is far harder.

None of this excuses waste. Data center water use — particularly for cooling — is a legitimate, under-reported concern in water-stressed regions. But energy efficiency efforts should be aimed at the biggest targets first.

Quick calibrations

  • 🌍
    World final energy: ~410 EJ / year (IEA 2022). Electricity is ~80 EJ of that.
  • ❄️
    Cooling's trajectory: By 2050, cooling could be the single largest electricity end-use globally under high-warming scenarios.
  • 💡
    LED leverage: If all lighting worldwide switched to LED today, the savings would equal roughly 5× the current data center total.
  • 💧
    Water, not watts: A single large data center can use 3–5 million gallons of water per day for cooling — a genuinely local environmental stress.
  • 🚂
    Transport comparison: Global road transport used ~27,000 TWh of energy equivalent in 2022 — mostly liquid fuel, invisible to electricity statistics.

Expert perspectives

"The 10% figure has been circulating since around 2008 and was dubious even then. Good analysis requires good data — and good data shows a far more nuanced picture."

— Energy systems researcher, paraphrased from IEA commentary

"Efficiency gains in data center hardware have largely offset the explosive growth in computing demand. The amount of compute per kilowatt-hour has roughly doubled every 2–3 years."

— Paraphrased from Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, 2020 report

"Space cooling is now the fastest-growing source of electricity demand globally. In some projections it overtakes industrial use by mid-century. It deserves far more policy attention than it currently receives."

— IEA Cooling Report synthesis, 2023
